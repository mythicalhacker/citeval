# A Literature-Grounded Ordinal Rubric for Grading Claim–Source Substantive Support

## TL;DR
- Adopt a **6-point ordinal scale** — Fully Supported (5) · Supported-with-Overreach (4) · Partially Supported (3) · Technically-True-but-Misleading (2) · Unsupported-for-the-Claim (1) · Contradicted (0) — anchored in the biomedical citation-accuracy taxonomy (major vs. minor quotation error), Greenberg's citation-distortion vocabulary, the Boutron/Yavchitz spin severity classes, GRADE indirectness, and the SUPPORTS/REFUTES/NEI claim-verification datasets.
- The three hard middle grades are separated by **ordered operational tests**: overreach = correct direction but inflated magnitude/scope; partial = only some conjuncts of a multi-part claim hold; misleading = every word defensible but framing creates a false impression (spin). Two traps must be coded explicitly: a correctly-scoped narrow claim is Fully Supported (never overreach), and a method/background/passing citation is out of scope for support grading.
- The resveratrol case (animal finding cited for a human clinical-benefit claim) lands at grade 1–2 via GRADE indirect-population + indirect-outcome and the preclinical-overextrapolation literature; the "B03" hedged case (an effect shown "including in non-human primates and humans") is Fully Supported because it accurately states the demonstrated scope. Scope failure should **cap** the grade; retraction is a separate orthogonal gate.

## Key Findings

**1. The biomedical citation-accuracy literature already encodes the top and bottom of the scale.** de Lacey, Record & Wade (BMJ 1985) originated the major/minor distinction, defining a severely flawed quotation as one "seriously misrepresenting or bearing no resemblance to the original source," and minor errors as those that "misled or could mislead, but the errors were not sufficiently serious to destroy or fundamentally to alter the meaning." Evans, Nadjari & Burchell (JAMA 1990, "Quotational and reference accuracy in surgical journals") applied the same major/minor grading. The systematic review by Jergas & Baethge (PeerJ 2015) crystallizes the operational rule: a **major quotation error** exists when "the reference contradicted, failed to substantiate, or was irrelevant to the author's assertion" (quoting Luo et al. 2013), and "the guiding principle … is that a major quotation error is not at all in accordance with the claim of the authors," whereas minor errors are "inconsistencies and factual errors not severe enough to contradict a statement by citing authors." Verification is performed by two independent coders checking each quotation against the retrieved original. This yields a defensible three-way spine: accurate (grade 5) / minor error (grades 4–3) / major error, which itself splits into "fails to substantiate" (grade 1) and "contradicts" (grade 0).

**2. Greenberg (BMJ 2009) supplies the vocabulary for overreach beyond binary error.** His "Vocabulary of citation distortions" box names the mechanisms by which a hedged finding becomes overstated authority: **citation bias** ("systematic ignoring of papers that contain content conflicting with a claim"), **amplification** ("expansion of a belief system without data" — citing papers that contain no primary data on the claim), and **invention**, which subdivides into **citation diversion** ("citing content but claiming it has a different meaning, thereby diverting its implications"), **citation transmutation** ("the conversion of hypothesis into fact through the act of citation alone"), **dead-end citation** ("support of a claim with citation to papers that do not contain content addressing the claim"), **back-door invention**, and **title invention**. Greenberg documents the exact overreach pattern the rubric must catch: a source stating something "may represent early changes of IBM" (hypothesis) later cited as established fact. His archetypal exaggeration case — three papers reporting β-amyloid in a "few fibres" cited as having "confirmed" the claim, generating 7,848 supportive citation paths — is the canonical grade-4 (overreach) instance; dead-end citation maps to grade 1.

**3. The spin literature (Boutron, Yavchitz, Chiu/Bero) supplies the "technically true but misleading" grade and its severity ordering.** Boutron, Dutton, Ravaud & Altman (JAMA 2010) defined spin as "specific reporting strategies … to highlight that the experimental treatment is beneficial, despite a statistically nonsignificant difference for the primary outcome, or to distract the reader from statistically nonsignificant results," and graded conclusion-level spin into none/low/moderate/high on four indicators. Boutron & Ravaud (PNAS 2018, "Misrepresentation and distortion of research in biomedical literature") generalized the definition to "reporting that fails to faithfully reflect the nature and range of findings and that could affect the impression the results produce in readers," organizing spin as misreporting the methods, misreporting the results, misinterpretation, and other types. Yavchitz et al. (J Clin Epidemiol 2016) identified 39 spin types grouped into **(1) misleading reporting, (2) misleading interpretation, and (3) inappropriate extrapolation**, and — critically for a rubric — ranked them by severity via a Q-sort of 122 Cochrane members: the three most severe were "(1) recommendations for clinical practice not supported by findings in the conclusion, (2) misleading title, and (3) selective reporting." Chiu, Grundy & Bero (PLoS Biology 2017) note the two commonest mechanisms are "detracting from statistically nonsignificant results and inappropriately using causal language" — i.e., causal claims on correlational data, a canonical grade-2 pattern. Yavchitz et al. (PLoS Med 2012) showed spin propagates from abstracts into press releases and news — the real-world analogue of Greenberg's amplification.

**4. Claim-verification datasets fix the anchors but are documented as too coarse for the middle.** FEVER (Thorne et al. 2018), SciFact (Wadden et al. 2020), HealthVer (Sarrouti et al. 2021), and CLIMATE-FEVER (Diggelmann et al. 2020) all use SUPPORTS/REFUTES/NOT ENOUGH INFO. SciFact's operational definitions: SUPPORTS = evidence confirms the claim (same finding); REFUTES = evidence contradicts (opposite finding); NOINFO = evidence tangentially related but does not verify or refute; each SUPPORTS/REFUTES pairing requires annotator-selected rationale sentences. A documented shortcoming: NEI means different things across datasets (in SciFact, no candidate abstract has suitable evidence; elsewhere, present-but-inconclusive evidence). PUBHEALTH adds a MIXED label "denoting a claim that consists of multiple factual statements with opposite veracity labels" — a direct dataset precedent for a Partially-Supported grade. CLIMATE-FEVER adds DISPUTED for "a mixture of SUPPORTS and REFUTES evidence items," and its authors note the FEVER majority-vote aggregation "is too naïve as, in many cases, retrieved evidence covers only some facets of the claim" — an explicit admission that ternary labels miss partial support. Newer graded work (the RIGOURATE overclaiming/exaggeration benchmark; FactReview's four-way Supported/Partially-supported/In-conflict/Inconclusive) confirms the field is moving toward graded overreach labels; FactReview's own definition ("Partially supported when only part of a decomposed claim is supported, or when support holds for some tasks, datasets, or metrics but not others") matches this rubric's grade 3.

**5. GRADE indirectness gives a principled, four-way scope axis for "right finding, wrong population/dose/outcome."** Guyatt et al. (J Clin Epidemiol 2011, "GRADE guidelines: 8. Rating the quality of evidence — indirectness") state: "Evidence can be indirect in one of four ways. First, patients may differ from those of interest (the term applicability is often used for this form of indirectness). Secondly, the intervention tested may differ from the intervention of interest. … Thirdly, outcomes may differ from those of primary interest — for instance, surrogate outcomes that are not themselves important, but measured in the presumption that changes in the surrogate reflect changes in an outcome important to patients. A fourth type of indirectness … occurs when clinicians must choose between interventions that have not been tested in head-to-head comparisons." On surrogates GRADE is explicit: "In general, the use of a surrogate outcome requires rating down the quality of evidence by one, or even two, levels," and "evidence based on surrogate outcomes should usually trigger rating down." Species (animal-vs-human) is the strongest form of indirect population. These four domains map directly onto scope axes (population/species, intervention/dose, comparator, outcome) and provide the citable basis for grading a claim whose direction is right but whose population/dose/outcome differs from the source.

## Details

### A. The ordinal scale (6 points)

For each grade: a one-sentence definition; the inclusion/exclusion boundary with each neighbor; and worked biomedical examples adapted from the cited literature.

---

**Grade 5 — Fully Supported.**
*Definition:* The cited source directly establishes the claim at the claim's stated scope, direction, and magnitude; every asserted element is substantiated by a rationale sentence in the source.
*Includes:* Verbatim-accurate paraphrase; correctly-hedged claims; correctly narrow claims (see Trap 1). Corresponds to SciFact SUPPORTS (Cohen's κ = 0.75 in Wadden et al. 2020) and to an "accurate quotation" in de Lacey/Evans.
*Excludes (vs. 4):* Any assertion of *more* than the source shows — broader population, larger effect, causal language on associational data, or dropping the source's hedges — drops to 4.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "In genetically heterogeneous mice on a standard diet, resveratrol did not extend lifespan." Cited finding: Pearson et al. (Cell Metabolism 2008, "Resveratrol Delays Age-Related Deterioration and Mimics Transcriptional Aspects of Dietary Restriction without Extending Life Span") — "mice fed a standard diet did not live longer when treated with resveratrol beginning at 12 months of age." (b) The B03-style hedged case (see Case 2). (c) Claim: "Resveratrol increased survival of mice on a high-calorie diet." Cited: Baur et al. (Nature 2006, "Resveratrol improves health and survival of mice on a high-calorie diet") — verbatim: "Here we show that resveratrol shifts the physiology of middle-aged mice on a high-calorie diet towards that of mice on a standard diet and significantly increases their survival."

**Grade 4 — Supported-with-Overreach.**
*Definition:* The claim's direction is correct and the source is genuinely on-point, but the claim asserts more than the source supports — inflated magnitude, generalized scope, hardened certainty, or a hypothesis restated as a finding.
*Includes:* Greenberg citation diversion and citation transmutation; Yavchitz "misleading interpretation" of moderate severity; Boutron conclusion spin at low–moderate level; a minor quotation error (de Lacey sense: meaning recognizable but altered) that inflates rather than merely garbles.
*Excludes (vs. 5):* The source's own hedge/scope is preserved → 5. *Excludes (vs. 3):* The claim is single-proposition and the source addresses all of it (so it is not "partial"); the defect is degree, not coverage. *Excludes (vs. 2):* The overreach is a substantive over-assertion the source's data do not reach, not merely a framing/emphasis effect on true statements.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "β-amyloid accumulation precedes other abnormalities in inclusion body myositis" cited to a source that stated this only as a hypothesis ("may represent early changes") — Greenberg's citation-transmutation case. (b) Claim: "Resveratrol confers cardioprotective benefit" cited only to a trial showing improvement in a surrogate biomarker (e.g., endothelial function) — direction right, patient-important outcome overreached (GRADE surrogate indirectness caps at 4). (c) A drug "reduces mortality" cited to a trial with a nonsignificant mortality trend but a significant secondary endpoint (Boutron spin).

**Grade 3 — Partially Supported.**
*Definition:* The claim is multi-part (conjunctive, or spans multiple outcomes/populations/conditions) and the source substantiates some conjuncts but not others.
*Includes:* PUBHEALTH MIXED; CLIMATE-FEVER "evidence covers only some facets of the claim"; FactReview "only part of a decomposed claim is supported."
*Excludes (vs. 4):* The defect is coverage/decomposition (some parts unsupported), not degree of a single assertion. *Excludes (vs. 2):* At least one conjunct is genuinely, fully supported and the unsupported parts are simply not established (not true-but-misframed). *Excludes (vs. 1):* A material part is actually supported.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "Drug X reduces pain and improves function" cited to a trial reporting only a pain endpoint. (b) Claim: "Intervention works in adults and children" cited to an adults-only trial. (c) Claim: "Resveratrol improves motor coordination and bone density in aged mice" cited to Pearson et al. — both conjuncts supported → this would be 5; but "improves motor coordination and extends lifespan" cited to the same paper is partial (first supported, second refuted) and is better handled by decomposition (see Composition).

**Grade 2 — Technically-True-but-Misleading (Spin).**
*Definition:* Every individual statement is literally defensible against the source, but the framing, selection, or causal language creates an impression the source's results do not support.
*Includes:* Boutron/Yavchitz spin — inappropriate focus on statistically significant secondary or subgroup outcomes, interpreting a nonsignificant primary result as equivalence, causal language on correlational/observational data (Chiu/Bero), misleading emphasis. Corresponds to "misleading reporting/interpretation" that does not cross into a false proposition.
*Excludes (vs. 3):* No proposition is unsupported — the words are all true; the defect is impression, not coverage. *Excludes (vs. 4):* The claim does not over-assert a specific substantive fact beyond the data; it re-frames true facts. *Excludes (vs. 1):* The literal content is defensible, so it is not simply unsupported.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "Resveratrol supplementation improved antioxidant capacity" (literally true for one marker) presented as a general antioxidant benefit while omitting the null results for other markers. (b) A subgroup-only benefit framed as a general treatment effect. (c) Causal "X reduces risk of Y" cited to a cross-sectional association.

**Grade 1 — Unsupported-for-the-Claim.**
*Definition:* The source neither substantiates nor contradicts the claim — it is off-point (wrong population/species/outcome such that the claim is not addressed), addresses a different question, or contains no data on the claim.
*Includes:* Major quotation error of the "fails to substantiate / irrelevant" type (Luo/Jergas); Greenberg dead-end citation and amplification; SciFact NOINFO ("tangentially related but doesn't verify or refute"). The resveratrol-clinical case typically lands here when a mouse finding is cited for an unqualified human clinical-benefit claim.
*Excludes (vs. 2):* The claim is not merely misframed — the source does not support the literal content. *Excludes (vs. 0):* The source does not contradict the claim; it is silent/irrelevant, not opposed.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "Resveratrol extends human lifespan / confers clinical benefit in patients" cited only to Baur et al. (mouse, high-calorie diet, survival) — indirect population (species) plus indirect outcome; the human clinical claim is not addressed. (b) Greenberg's dead-end citation: a claim "supported" by a paper containing no statement on the claim. (c) A method paper cited as if it reported a headline finding (usually excluded entirely under Trap 2).

**Grade 0 — Contradicted.**
*Definition:* The source's finding is opposite in direction to the claim; the source refutes or directly undercuts the claim.
*Includes:* SciFact/FEVER REFUTES ("opposite finding"); de Lacey/Evans major error where "the reference contradicted … the author's assertion."
*Excludes (vs. 1):* The source actively opposes the claim rather than being silent.
*Worked examples:* (a) Claim: "Resveratrol extends lifespan in normal mice" cited to Pearson et al. or the NIA Interventions Testing Program, which found it did **not** extend lifespan in standard-diet / genetically heterogeneous mice. (b) Claim: "β-amyloid is specific to inclusion body myositis muscle" cited to a paper (Askanas/Sarkozi) reporting the protein present across many other muscle diseases (Greenberg's suppressed critical data).

### B. Boundary decision rules for the hard adjacencies

The three-way middle (4 vs. 3 vs. 2) is the adjudication risk. Apply these ordered operational tests, each citable:

1. **Direction test (separates 0 from everything above).** Is the source's finding opposite to the claim? If yes → 0 (SciFact REFUTES; de Lacey "contradicted").
2. **On-point test (separates 1 from 2–5).** Does the source contain data/finding that actually bears on this claim's population, intervention, and outcome? If no → 1 (major "fails to substantiate"; Greenberg dead-end citation; GRADE fatal indirectness). This is where the resveratrol-for-humans case falls.
3. **Decomposition test (identifies 3).** Decompose the claim into atomic propositions (FactReview-style decomposition). If it is genuinely multi-part and the source supports some but not all conjuncts → 3 (PUBHEALTH MIXED; CLIMATE-FEVER "only some facets").
4. **Literal-truth test (separates 2 from 4).** Is every atomic statement literally defensible against the source? If yes but the framing/selection/causal language misleads → 2 (spin: technically true but misleading). If instead a specific substantive element over-asserts the data (bigger effect, broader population, hypothesis-as-fact) → 4 (overreach/citation diversion).
5. **Hedge-preservation test (separates 4 from 5).** Does the claim preserve the source's scope and hedges? If yes → 5. If it drops a hedge or generalizes → 4.

Operational summary matching the task's proposed tests: *"direction correct but magnitude/scope inflated" = overreach (4); "only part of a multi-part claim supported" = partial (3); "every word defensible but framing creates a false impression" = misleading/spin (2).* Each has a named literature basis (Greenberg diversion/transmutation; PUBHEALTH/CLIMATE-FEVER partial; Boutron/Yavchitz/Chiu spin).

### C. The two traps

**Trap 1 — Correctly-scoped narrow claims are NOT overreach.** Overreach (grade 4) requires the claim to assert *more* than the source supports. A claim that accurately states a narrow scope — "in mice," "whether this holds in humans is unknown," or "including non-human primates and humans" — asserts exactly what was shown and is therefore **Fully Supported (5)**, regardless of how narrow the source is. The principle is GRADE indirectness applied in reverse: indirectness lowers certainty only when the claim's target differs from what was studied; if the claim's target *is* what was studied, there is no indirectness to penalize. The spin literature reinforces this: Boutron/Yavchitz define inappropriate extrapolation as *unjustified* extrapolation — a claim that explicitly confines itself to the studied scope makes no extrapolation and cannot be spin. Adjudication rule: locate the scope qualifiers in the claim sentence; if they match the source's population/intervention/outcome, grade on substance alone and do not apply any indirectness cap.

**Trap 2 — Method/background/passing citations are OUT OF SCOPE for support grading.** The citation-intent literature operationally distinguishes substantiating citations from non-substantiating ones. SciCite (Cohan et al. 2019, 11,020 citations) classifies every citation as **Background** ("the citation states, mentions, or points to the background information giving more context about a problem, concept, approach, topic, or importance of the problem in the field"), **Method** ("making use of a method, tool, approach or dataset"), or **Result comparison** ("comparison of the paper's results/findings with the results/findings of other work"). Teufel, Siddharthan & Tidhar (2006) similarly separate Weakness / Contrast / Positive(usage) / Neutral functions (Cohen's κ = 0.72), and the ACL-ARC scheme (Jurgens et al. 2018) adds Uses/Motivation/Extension/Future. Support-grading applies only to citations whose function is to substantiate a knowledge claim (SciCite Result-comparison, or a Background citation used to assert a factual finding). A citation used purely for a method, dataset, tool, or generic background pointer should be flagged **N/A — non-substantiating** and excluded from the ordinal scale. Adjudication rule: classify citation intent first; only grade citations that assert the cited work's finding as support for a factual claim.

### D. Composition with objective axes

**Scope failure should CAP the substantive grade, not merely annotate it.** GRADE treats indirectness as a reason to *rate down* certainty by one or two levels, and states surrogate-outcome evidence "should usually trigger rating down." Translated to this rubric: when a claim's direction matches the source but the population/species, intervention/dose, comparator, or outcome differs materially, the scope mismatch imposes a ceiling. Recommended capping logic (a design decision, flagged as such): a single major indirectness domain (e.g., surrogate outcome for a patient-important claim) caps at grade 4; a fatal indirectness where the claim's target is simply not addressed (e.g., an animal finding for a human clinical-benefit claim = indirect population *and* indirect outcome simultaneously) caps at grade 1. GRADE's guidance that multiple simultaneous indirectness domains compound supports treating species+outcome mismatch as fatal. This preserves a clean separation: an orthogonal, objective **scope axis** (population/species · intervention/dose · comparator · outcome type = surrogate vs. patient-important) records *which* indirectness applies; the substantive grade is then capped accordingly. Recording both (grade + scope-axis reason) lets the adjudication agent cite *why* a case is capped.

**Retraction is a separate orthogonal gate, not a point on the support scale.** A source can be perfectly on-point and still be retracted. The post-retraction-citation literature shows retracted work continues to be cited as valid: Schneider et al. (Scientometrics 2020), analyzing the Matsuyama fabricated-data case, found that of 112 post-retraction citations only five (4.5%) mentioned the retraction; Hsiao & Schneider (Quantitative Science Studies, MIT Press, 2021;2(4):1144), analyzing 13,252 post-retraction citation contexts across 7,813 retracted PubMed papers, found only 722 (5.4%) acknowledged the retraction. Recommended handling: a **retraction flag** is a binary gate applied independently of the substantive grade. A claim substantively "supported" by a retracted source should retain its computed support grade *for auditing* but carry a hard retraction flag that voids its evidentiary value — unless the citing text explicitly acknowledges the retraction (e.g., cites it as an example of problematic science, which is itself a non-substantiating Background/Contrast intent under Trap 2). This keeps "does the text support the claim" separate from "is the source itself valid."

### Resolution of the two named cases

**RESVERATROL-STYLE case (animal finding → human clinical claim): grade 1 (Unsupported-for-the-claim), or 2 if framed as true-but-misleading.** Claim: "Resveratrol improves metabolic health / extends lifespan, supporting its use for human health." Cited finding: Baur et al. (Nature 2006), "Resveratrol improves health and survival of mice on a high-calorie diet," or Pearson et al. (Cell Metabolism 2008). Justification: (i) GRADE indirect population — species difference (mouse→human) is the strongest applicability failure; (ii) GRADE indirect outcome — mouse survival/biomarkers are surrogates for human clinical benefit, which GRADE says "requires rating down … by one, or even two, levels"; (iii) the preclinical-translation literature quantifies why the extrapolation is unjustified — Hackam & Redelmeier (JAMA 2006), analyzing ~2,000 highly cited animal studies from seven top journals (1980–2000), found only 37% translated at the level of human randomized trials, and Ineichen et al. (PLoS Biology 2024, umbrella review of 122 articles, 54 diseases, 367 interventions) found only 5% of animal-tested therapeutic interventions obtain regulatory approval for human applications. Because the human clinical claim is *not addressed* by a mouse study, it fails the on-point test → grade 1. If the citing text uses literally-true mouse language to imply human benefit, it is spin → grade 2. It never earns 4/5 because scope failure caps it. This is exactly Greenberg's "citation bias to justify models" pattern — animal models cited to amplify a human claim.

**B03-STYLE hedged case (effect shown "including in non-human primates and humans"): grade 5 (Fully Supported).** Claim: "Effect E has been demonstrated including in non-human primates and humans." If the cited source did demonstrate E across those species, the claim states exactly the demonstrated scope. No indirectness applies (the claim's target matches the studied populations), so there is nothing to rate down; no extrapolation is made, so it cannot be spin (Boutron/Yavchitz). Per Trap 1, grade on substance alone → 5. The narrowness or breadth of scope is irrelevant; accuracy of scope is what matters.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt the 6-point scale as the `substantive_support` label space**, with integer anchors 5→0 and the one-sentence definitions above. Ship the five ordered boundary tests (Section B) as the adjudication decision tree so a grader cites the *test* that fired, not a gestalt judgment.
2. **Require the adjudication agent to cite, for every grade, (a) the rationale sentence(s) in the source** (SciFact-style evidence selection) **and (b) the boundary test** that placed it at that grade rather than the neighbor. This is what delivers "cite why, not assert."
3. **Implement two orthogonal axes alongside the grade:** a scope/indirectness axis (population/species · intervention/dose · comparator · outcome type) using GRADE's four domains, and a binary retraction gate. Apply the capping logic (single major indirectness → cap 4; fatal/compound indirectness → cap 1) and record the cap reason.
4. **Gate on citation intent first.** Run a SciCite-style Background/Method/Result classifier (or a rule) and mark Method/passing citations N/A before support-grading. This prevents the most common false-positive "unsupported" errors.
5. **Calibrate against the named literatures for inter-rater reliability.** Seed the graded corpus with adapted worked examples from Greenberg (diversion/transmutation → grade 4; dead-end → grade 1), Boutron/Yavchitz (spin → grade 2), PUBHEALTH/CLIMATE-FEVER (mixed → grade 3), and the resveratrol/B03 cases (1–2 vs. 5). Measure agreement; the design target (≥80% exact agreement, ≤1 adjacent-grade deviation) is plausible given that SciFact ternary labeling reports Cohen's κ = 0.75, FEVER reports Fleiss' κ = 0.6841, and Teufel citation-function annotation reports κ = 0.72 — and the finer grades here are backed by explicit operational tests.
   - **Thresholds that change the design:** if exact agreement falls below 80% or off-by-two errors exceed 5%, collapse the two hardest-to-separate grades (most likely 4 and 2) into a single "over-claim" grade, yielding a 5-point scale; conversely, if graders reliably separate spin sub-types, extend the scale by splitting grade 2 along Yavchitz's misleading-reporting vs. misleading-interpretation vs. inappropriate-extrapolation axes.
6. **Document design decisions vs. sourced facts.** The capping thresholds and the exact mapping of Greenberg/Boutron categories to integer grades are design choices (flagged here); the category *definitions* and severity *orderings* are sourced. Keep that distinction in the rubric documentation.

## Caveats
- **The scale's midpoints are synthesized, not lifted whole from one source.** No single paper offers a validated 6-point claim-vs-source scale; the anchors (5, 1, 0) come from citation-accuracy plus SUPPORTS/REFUTES/NEI, while the middle (4, 3, 2) is assembled from Greenberg, the spin literature, and partial-support dataset labels. The ordering is defensible, but the *number* of points and the capping arithmetic are design decisions.
- **Inter-rater reliability figures cited (Cohen's κ = 0.75 SciFact, Fleiss' κ = 0.6841 FEVER, κ = 0.72 Teufel) are for coarser or different tasks.** They bound expectations but do not guarantee ≥80% agreement on this finer scale without calibration. Treat the 80%/adjacent-grade target as a design goal to be validated, not an established result.
- **"Spin" (grade 2) vs. "overreach" (grade 4) is the genuine reliability risk.** Both involve overstatement; the literal-truth test is the discriminator, but borderline causal-language cases can plausibly be coded either way. Expect most disagreements here and treat it as the first candidate for grade-collapsing.
- **Translation figures are precise but field-dependent.** The 37% animal-to-human-RCT figure (Hackam & Redelmeier, JAMA 2006) and the 5% animal-to-approval figure (Ineichen et al., PLoS Biology 2024) are well-sourced, but exact percentages vary by disease domain and definition of "translation."
- **The resveratrol human-RCT negative evidence** rests on meta-analyses reporting null or inconsistent effects on metabolic and oxidative-stress markers; the specific verbatim conclusion of any single meta-analysis should be re-verified against its primary text before being quoted verbatim in the corpus (one candidate source contained a probable typographical error in its indexed abstract).
- **Retraction handling assumes reliable retraction metadata.** Given documented failures of digital libraries to surface retraction status, the retraction gate is only as good as the underlying retraction database feeding it.
